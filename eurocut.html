Crown height and side profile

Old European cuts usually have high crowns between 15% and 22%. Diamond 1 has the highest crown at 20.0%, giving it a distinct rounded vintage dome profile that dramatically magnifies internal facet reflections. Diamond 3 sits just under it at 19.0%, keeping the antique sculptural presence while spreading wider across the finger (6.45 mm). Diamond 2 is the flattest at 18.5%, closer to a transitional cut from the 1920s.

Angles and light return

Steep antique crowns need the right bottom angle so light does not leak out the bottom. Diamond 3 pairs a 39.0° crown with a 40.1° pavilion. This combo returns light efficiently, creating broad flashes of color and clean contrast between light and dark facets. Diamond 1 pairs a 40.1° crown with a 39.1° pavilion, which keeps the center bright when viewed directly face up.

The open culet & Kozibe Effect

Old European cuts have a flat polished octagonal facet at the bottom tip instead of a point (~7%–9% diameter). When light strikes this flat opening, it bounces internally across opposing pavilion mains, projecting an echoing halo of secondary culet images around the outer rim (the Kozibe effect). Diamonds 1 and 3 both exhibit clear, open culets with crisp perimeter Kozibe satellites. Diamond 2 has a tighter bottom opening, so its culet reflection is more subtle.

Crystal clarity and color

All three stones are lab-grown CVD Type IIa diamonds graded D color and VVS2 clarity. In 360 video, the diamond material looks clear and water-like. None of the three show brown tints, internal grain lines, or cloudy patches. The facet edges are sharp and clean across all three stones.

Maltese cross and contrast dynamics

The signature pattern of an Old European cut is an 8-petal rosette centered under the table. Eight broad pavilion main facets reflect light through steep crown facets (~38° to 40°).

•
4 cardinal petals (0°, 90°, 180°, 270°): When viewed directly face up, these four facets reflect the viewer head shadow, producing dark contrast arms.
•
4 diagonal petals (45°, 135°, 225°, 315°): Set between the cardinal petals, these four facets return bright ambient light, creating alternating light and dark pattern.

8 radial rays (0° to 315°)

The optical reticle projects 8 lines from the open culet to the girdle at 45-degree intervals.

•
Symmetry check: In a symmetrical cut, each ray bisects a crown star facet and the center of a Maltese petal. Hand-cut variance shows up as slight angle offsets.
•
Tracking rotation: During rotation, the degree markers help track when opposing facet pairs flash or go dark together.

Central light return and facet scintillation

A shallow pavilion under 38% causes light to leak out the back, leaving a dull gray center window.

All three diamonds pair crown angles (38.1° to 40.1°) and pavilion angles (39.1° to 40.1°) to keep internal light return high. As the stone tilts, facets switch cleanly between light and shadow.

Kozibe effect: open culet reflections

When the culet is cut flat, light bouncing inside the pavilion creates multiple small reflections around the edge of the table.

Diamond 1 and Diamond 3 show distinct culet reflections that give visual depth when the stone moves.

1. Turn on optical reticle

2. Check 0° face-up symmetry

3. Check scintillation motion

Click Swing in the top bar to oscillate ±12°. Watch how the facets alternate between bright flash and contrast shadow.
